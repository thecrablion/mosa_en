What to wear, by occasion

Eight events that eat up the most searching, answered with the same three questions: what does the room require, how long will you be in it, and who is watching.

Every outfit question is three questions in disguise. What does the place require - the floor, the temperature, the security desk, the seating. How long will you be in it - forty minutes in a pew and eight hours on a jury chair are different garments. Who is watching - a jury, your manager, the mother of the graduate, the person in the front row of a funeral.

Most style advice answers the third question only, which is why so many outfits photograph well and fail by hour two. So each page here starts from the room and the clock, and then gets to the silhouette: the shoes that work on grass, the layer you cannot leave in the car, the bag that clears security, the color that keeps you out of the photograph for the wrong reason.

There is one more thing all eight have in common, and it is the reason this site exists: before you buy, see it on your own body. The try-on tool takes a photo of you and puts the garment on it, so a dress you are guessing about stops being a guess.

Where to start. Pick the event, not the aesthetic. If your event is not on the list, the dress-code table at the bottom still answers it: cocktail, black tie, smart casual and festive are all decoded here.

The three questions

Room, clock, audience. Answer them in that order and most outfits decide themselves - which is why a concert outfit is a venue problem and a funeral outfit is a standing-and-sitting problem before it is a color problem.

The one-elevated-piece rule

Across all eight events, the outfit that works is a normal outfit plus one deliberate thing: the sleeve, the earring, the shoe. Two elevated pieces reads as a costume, zero reads as not trying.

See it before you buy

Color and length are the two things a product photo lies about. The try-on puts the garment on your photo, which is the only way to check a midi length when you are not standing in it.

Dress-code decoder: <b>black tie</b> means floor-length or a dark cocktail dress; <b>cocktail</b> means knee to midi with one shine; <b>smart casual</b> means a jacket over something plain; <b>festive</b> allows color and texture; <b>elegant casual</b> is the tricky one - nicer fabric, simple cut, no theme.

Questions

Why is there no page for 'date night' or 'brunch'?

Because those are aesthetic questions, not dress-code questions: nobody is judging the room's rules, so a list of outfits is a mood board. This site answers events that come with a rule to satisfy - a venue, a security desk, a jury, a dress code.

What is the one thing that works at all eight events?

A solid color, mid-length, closed shoes you can stand in, and one deliberate accessory. Boring on purpose: boring is what survives a room you have never seen.

Do these pages assume a size or an age?

No. Fit advice for petite, plus size or over 60 is not what this site does; here the variables are the room, the hours and the audience, which are the same for everyone.

How do I know if my outfit is too much?

Take a photo of it with your phone camera, at the distance other people will stand. If the outfit is the first thing you notice in a room where you are not the guest of honor, remove one thing.
